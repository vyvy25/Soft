# Writing Guideline

A general guide for writing product and marketing copy. Use it across projects.

## Core Principle

Write to bring value to the reader, not to describe what you built. Every sentence should answer one of three questions: what is it, why does it matter to me, what do I do next. If a sentence doesn't move the reader closer to one of those answers, cut it.

## Voice

### Confident, not loud
State facts. Don't sell them. A confident voice trusts the reader to recognize value without being told to. Avoid hype words that try to do the work for you.

### Warm, not casual
Friendly enough to feel human. Professional enough to be trusted. The reader is a busy adult, not a buddy. No slang, no emojis in body copy, no jokes about the product.

### Specific, not vague
Replace adjectives with proof. "Fast" is opinion. "Under 2 seconds" is fact. Numbers, names, and concrete examples beat sweeping claims every time.

### Active, not passive
The subject does the action. "We send your money" beats "Your money is sent." Active voice is shorter, clearer, and more honest about who does what.

## Tone

Tone shifts with context. Voice stays the same.

| Context | Tone shift |
|---|---|
| Hero and marketing | Aspirational, energetic |
| Product and feature copy | Clear, practical |
| Errors and warnings | Calm, helpful |
| Success and confirmation | Brief, reassuring |
| Legal and policy | Plain, complete |
| Empty states | Encouraging, directional |

When in doubt, lean clear over clever.

## Sentence Length

Short. Most sentences under 20 words. Read every sentence aloud. If you run out of breath, cut it in half.

Vary the rhythm. Three short sentences in a row feels choppy. One long sentence after two short ones feels deliberate. Use the long one to explain, the short ones to land.

One idea per sentence. If you find yourself using "and" twice, you have two sentences hiding inside one.

## Word Choice

### Use simple words
Use the shorter word when the meaning is the same. "Use" beats "utilize." "Help" beats "facilitate." "Show" beats "demonstrate." Smart writing sounds simple, not clever.

### Cut filler
Adverbs are usually filler. Very, really, simply, easily, basically, actually, literally, just. Most can be deleted with no loss.

### Avoid empty words
These words promise something but say nothing: revolutionary, game changing, world class, cutting edge, next generation, best in class, holistic, synergy, leverage, robust, seamless. If you remove them, the sentence still means the same thing. So remove them.

### Use second person
"You" and "your" make the reader the subject. "Users" and "customers" turn them into a category. Talk to one person at a time.

### Verbs over nouns
"We help you decide" beats "We provide decision support." Verbs move. Nouns sit still.

## Headlines

A good headline does one of three things: states an outcome the reader wants, names a problem the reader has, or makes a specific promise. It does not describe the product.

Weak: "A platform for modern teams"
Strong: "Ship faster without breaking things"

Weak: "Innovative payment solutions"
Strong: "Get paid in 12 currencies, settle the same day"

Aim for 6 to 12 words. Two lines maximum. Cut every word that doesn't earn its place.

## Subheads

The subhead expands the headline with one specific proof or detail. One sentence, under 25 words. If the headline promises speed, the subhead names the number. If the headline promises ease, the subhead shows the steps.

## Body Copy

Lead with the conclusion. The reader scans before they read. Put the most useful sentence first, then explain.

Group ideas. One paragraph, one idea. When the topic shifts, start a new paragraph, even a short one.

Show, don't tell. Instead of "easy to use," describe what makes it easy. Instead of "trusted," name who trusts it.

## Calls to Action

Buttons are promises. They tell the reader what will happen next. Two to four words. Verb first.

Strong: Open account, Start free trial, Talk to sales, See pricing
Weak: Learn more, Click here, Submit, Get started (if "started" with what is unclear)

Use first person when it fits the moment. "Start my trial" feels more committed than "Start your trial." Test which works for the brand.

## Microcopy

The small text matters more than the big text. It carries the brand into the parts of the product people actually use.

### Form labels
Short and clear. "Work email" not "Please enter your work email address." Place help text below the field, not inside it.

### Errors
Calm and useful. Name what went wrong, then how to fix it. Never blame the reader. "We couldn't find that account. Check the email and try again" beats "Invalid input."

### Empty states
A blank screen is a chance to teach. Say what goes here, why it's empty, and how to fill it. One sentence is usually enough.

### Success messages
Brief. The action speaks louder than the message. "Saved" is often enough. "Your changes have been successfully saved to your account" is too much.

### Tooltips
One sentence. Define the term or explain the choice. Don't sell, don't repeat the label.

## Numbers and Proof

Numbers earn trust. Use them whenever possible. Countries, currencies, customers, years, percentages, time saved.

Round numbers feel marketing. Specific numbers feel real. "Saves 4.2 hours a week" lands harder than "Saves about 4 hours a week."

Never invent numbers. If the data isn't ready, mark it `[NEEDS DATA]` and ask the source. Inventing a stat to make a sentence stronger is the fastest way to lose trust.

## Capitalization

Default to sentence case for headings, body, and microcopy. It reads more naturally and feels less corporate.

Title Case is acceptable for hero headlines and major section headers when the brand calls for a more heroic register. Pick one approach per project and stick to it.

ALL CAPS only for tiny labels (eyebrow text, badges). Never for full sentences.

## Punctuation

Use periods at the end of full sentences, including in body copy. Skip them on headlines, buttons, navigation, and short labels.

Use commas to separate ideas, not to dress up a sentence. If a comma is doing the work of a period, use the period.

Avoid exclamation marks. They sound like the writer is trying too hard. One per page, max, and only when something genuinely deserves it.

Avoid em dashes when a period or comma works. Em dashes can make writing feel breathless.

## Inclusive Writing

Write so anyone can read it. Avoid jargon, idioms, and cultural references that don't translate. Use gender neutral terms by default. "They" is fine for an unknown person.

Define acronyms on first use. Don't assume the reader knows internal terms.

## What to Avoid

Empty hype. "Revolutionary," "world class," "cutting edge."

Filler phrases. "At the end of the day," "in today's world," "more than ever before."

Hedging. "We believe," "we think," "we hope to." Either say it or don't.

Apologizing. "Unfortunately," "we're sorry to inform you" in places where a calm fact would do.

Selling inside the product. Once the user is in, stop pitching. Help them do their job.

## Line Endings
- All text blocks — headings, titles, descriptions, body copy — must never end with a single word or a two-word last line. Reword or trim until the last line has at least 3–4 words.
- Titles and headings: short enough to fit on one line at the target font size. Always verify at desktop width before finalising.

## Self Edit Checklist

Before any sentence ships, ask:

1. Would the reader understand this in five seconds?
2. Did I use a number, name, or concrete example?
3. Did I cut every adverb that wasn't load bearing?
4. Is the verb active?
5. Could a competitor copy paste this onto their site? If yes, make it more specific.
6. Read aloud: does it sound like a human or a brochure?

If a sentence fails one of these, rewrite it.
